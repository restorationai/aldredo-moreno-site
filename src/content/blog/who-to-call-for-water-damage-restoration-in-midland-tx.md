## When do you call a restoration company instead of a plumber?

Call a restoration company once water has touched drywall, flooring, insulation, or furniture, not just the broken pipe or fitting itself. A plumber fixes the part that failed. A restoration crew extracts standing water, measures how far moisture traveled behind walls and under flooring, and dries the structure before mold gets a foothold.

If the leak is still active, a plumber may need to shut off the supply line first. Many homeowners call both: a plumber to stop the source, and a restoration crew to start extraction and drying the same day. If you're not sure which problem you have, describe what you're seeing on the phone; the restoration dispatcher can usually tell you in under a minute whether you need a plumber on site too.

## What should you do before help arrives?

Shut off the water at the source if you can reach the valve safely, and cut power to any outlets or circuits in the affected area. Water and electricity in the same room is the one hazard that outranks the water damage itself.

If you smell gas, see water pooling near an electrical panel, or the water is rising fast from a sewer or septic backup, leave the area and call 911 or your utility company before anyone touches the water. Once it's safe:

- Move rugs, electronics, and anything irreplaceable out of the wet area
- Take a few photos or a short video of the standing water and visibly soaked materials, before anything is moved or dried
- Avoid walking through water if it's come from a toilet, sink drain, or any source that isn't clean supply water

## What happens when you call?

A restoration dispatcher asks a few quick questions, the water source, how long it's been running, and which rooms are affected, then sends a technician with extraction equipment and moisture meters. On arrival, the crew extracts standing water, checks moisture levels in walls and flooring with a meter (not just a visual check, since drywall can look dry while holding water behind it), and sets air movers and a dehumidifier to begin structural drying.

That moisture-meter documentation matters later: it's the record that shows an insurance adjuster exactly what got wet, how wet, and when drying started. ## When should insurance get involved?

Call your insurance carrier after mitigation has already started, not before, so drying doesn't sit delayed while you're on hold. Most homeowners' policies require you to act quickly to limit the damage, and a restoration crew's photos and moisture readings become the documentation your claim is built on.

Keep the claim number and your policy's deductible handy once a crew is on site; they can usually tell you, in plain terms, whether what they're seeing looks like a covered sudden loss (like a burst pipe) versus a gradual issue (like a slow roof leak) that your policy may treat differently.
