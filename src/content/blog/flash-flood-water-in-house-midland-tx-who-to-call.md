## What should you do in the first few minutes?

Cut power to any flooded rooms at the breaker panel, but only if the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping into standing water. If you're not sure, leave the power on and stay out of the flooded area entirely.

Treat any water that came in from outside, through a door, a window, a foundation crack, or up through the floor, as contaminated. Under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), floodwater that crosses yards, streets, and storm drains before reaching your house is Category 3, which means it can carry fuel residue, fertilizer, animal waste, or sewer overflow picked up along the way.

A few things to do while you wait for help:

- Photograph the water level and any visible damage before you move anything, for your insurance file.
- Keep kids and pets out of the affected rooms.
- Don't plug in or run any appliance that got wet, even if the outlet looks dry now.
- Move salvageable items (rugs, furniture legs, boxes) off the wet floor if you can do it safely.

## Who do you call first: the restoration crew, the utility, or your insurer?

Call 911 or your electric utility first only if you smell gas, see sparking, or spot a downed power line anywhere near the water. Otherwise, call a water damage crew before you call your insurance company.

Most homeowners policies expect you to limit further damage once a loss happens. That means getting water out and the structure drying is something you're supposed to start promptly, not something you wait on an adjuster to approve. Calling an insurer first, before anyone has stopped the water or documented the scene, usually just delays the part that actually protects your house.

Once extraction and drying are underway, that's the right time to open a claim. A restoration crew that documents moisture readings, photos, and the water's origin as they work hands you most of what an adjuster will ask for anyway.

## Does homeowners insurance cover flash flood water in Texas?

Usually not. Standard Texas homeowners policies generally cover sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, a burst supply line or a failed appliance hose, but water that flows in from outside during a flash flood typically needs a separate flood insurance policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood carrier](https://www.floodsmart.gov/get-insured/buy-a-policy).

That split catches a lot of homeowners off guard, because the damage inside the house can look identical either way. Check your actual policy and don't assume. The [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/consumer/disasters.html) and our own breakdown of [what homeowners insurance covers for water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) both walk through the distinction in more detail.

Standing water outdoors is also genuinely dangerous to wade through: it can hide open drains, downed lines, and debris, and [Ready.gov's flood guidance](https://www.ready.gov/floods) recommends never walking or driving through moving or standing floodwater if you can avoid it.
