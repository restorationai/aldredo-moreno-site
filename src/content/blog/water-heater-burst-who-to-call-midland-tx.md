## Who do you call first, a plumber or a water damage crew?

Call the plumber first to shut the tank down safely and diagnose whether it needs repair or a full replacement, since only a licensed plumber can legally reconnect gas or electrical lines to a new unit. Call the water damage crew right behind that call, ideally before you've even hung up, because every hour standing water sits on a slab floor or soaks into drywall and baseboards adds to what has to be replaced instead of dried.

Call the insurance company last, after extraction has already started. Insurers expect homeowners to begin mitigating a loss right away, and showing an adjuster that water removal was already underway supports the claim better than waiting for approval first. If you haven't worked through the first 24 hours of a water loss before, [this walkthrough](/blog/what-to-do-first-24-hours-water-damage/) covers the order of operations in more detail.

## What should you do in the first five minutes?

Shut off the cold water supply valve at the top of the tank, turn off the gas control valve or trip the breaker if the unit is electric, and open a hot water tap somewhere in the house to relieve pressure in the lines. Those three steps stop the flow and cut down the chance of a scald hazard or a gas smell while you wait on help.

Where the leak is coming from tells you something useful. A leak from the bottom of the tank usually means the tank itself has failed, rust-through or a cracked liner, and it will keep draining until it's empty or the supply is shut. A leak from the temperature and pressure relief valve on the side or top is different: it often signals the tank is overheating or over-pressurizing, and it may keep dripping even after the cold supply is off, because that valve is built to release water as a safety measure. Shutting the main line to the house stops a bottom-tank failure for good; a relief-valve leak may still need the plumber to isolate the unit.

## Where does the water go in a Midland slab-home water heater closet?

In most Midland homes the water heater sits in a garage, a hall closet, or an attic, and the water that escapes travels along whatever path is lowest, not where you'd expect to find it. Slab foundations don't drain water down and away the way a basement would, so it spreads sideways under baseboards, into adjoining closets, and under cabinet toe-kicks before it's obvious from the doorway.

A hall-closet unit typically pushes water into the hallway first, then under the baseboards of the nearest bedroom or bathroom. A garage unit sometimes stays contained by the slope of the garage floor toward the door, but just as often finds a gap under the firewall and into living space. An attic unit is the one that surprises people most: water travels through insulation and shows up as a ceiling stain in a room nowhere near the actual tank.

Midland's water also carries more dissolved minerals than a lot of the state, and the sediment that settles at the bottom of a tank over time is a common contributor to bottom-of-tank failures in this part of the Permian Basin. It's worth asking the plumber whether sediment played a role, since that affects how the replacement tank gets maintained going forward.

## What does the water damage crew do once they arrive?

A water damage crew's first job is extraction, pulling standing water out with truck-mounted or portable units before it migrates further under flooring and walls. From there they map moisture with a meter to see exactly how far the water traveled, since what's visible at the baseboard rarely marks the real edge of the damage.

Any wet drywall, baseboard, or flooring that won't dry back out gets removed below the waterline, following the [IICRC S500 water damage standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for how far materials need to come out versus dry in place. Air movers and a dehumidifier go in next to pull moisture out of the remaining structure, and the crew comes back over the following days to re-check readings until the materials test dry, not just look dry. ## Will insurance cover a burst water heater in Midland?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water heater failures, like a tank that splits or a line that bursts without warning, but they typically exclude damage from a slow leak that went unnoticed for weeks or months. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) describes this as the core distinction most policies draw: sudden and accidental versus gradual, and documentation is what proves which one actually happened.

Photograph the tank itself before it's moved, showing whether the leak came from the bottom or the relief valve. Photograph the water line on walls and flooring, the affected baseboards before they're removed, and keep any receipts tied to the loss. If coverage questions come up, the [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/) publishes consumer guidance on how claims like this typically get handled in the state, and our [plain-English coverage guide](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through what a standard policy usually pays for.
