## What should you do before help arrives?

Cut power to any flooded rooms at the breaker panel before you touch anything else, and never do it while standing in water. If the panel itself is wet or you're not sure which breakers feed the flooded area, leave the power on and wait for a professional.

Don't wade into moving water or water deeper than your ankles; flash flood water in West Texas can hide open manholes, debris, and downed lines you can't see. Skip the window AC unit or space heater sitting in standing water, and don't run a household vacuum on it either. Take photos of the water level and the rooms affected before you move anything. That documentation protects an insurance claim later, and it only takes a minute.

## Who do you call first: a plumber, the city, or a restoration company?

Call an emergency water removal and restoration company first when the water came from outside, like a flash flood; that's a contamination and extraction job, not a plumbing repair. Call 911 or your utility company only if you smell gas, see downed power lines, or hear arcing. Call your insurance agent after mitigation starts, not before, so the loss is already being controlled while the claim gets filed.

A plumber is the right call when the water is actively coming from a pipe, water heater, or supply line inside the home and hasn't stopped. A flash flood is different: the water's source is outside the house (a swollen arroyo, street runoff, or a creek like Beals Creek overtopping its banks), so there's no valve to shut to stop it. That's a job for extraction equipment and someone who can tell you honestly how far the water traveled into your walls and subfloor.

## What happens when you call?

Expect the dispatcher to ask how the water got in, roughly how much area is affected, and whether the power is still on in the flooded rooms. Have your address, a rough timeline, and your insurance company's name ready if you've already been in touch with them.

On arrival, a technician checks for electrical and structural hazards first, then starts extraction with truck-mounted or portable pumps. They'll use a moisture meter to check how far the water traveled behind drywall and under flooring, not just what's visible on the surface, and set air movers and dehumidifiers once standing water is gone. You should get a written scope of work before anything is torn out, which also becomes part of your insurance documentation.

## Is flash flood water actually contaminated?

Yes. Under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water restoration standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), water that floods in from outside, whether it's street runoff, a creek, or storm surge, is classified as Category 3, the most contaminated tier. It can carry sewage, chemicals, and bacteria picked up as it crossed yards, streets, and storm drains on its way into your house.

That classification is also why a sewage-grade cleanup is often the right scope for flash flood water, not a standard dry-out. Porous materials that soaked in Category 3 water, carpet pad, drywall below the waterline, insulation, usually need to come out rather than be dried in place. ## Does homeowners insurance cover flash flood water?

Usually not, at least not under a standard homeowners policy. Surface flooding, including flash flood water, is typically excluded from standard homeowners insurance and instead requires a separate flood policy, most often through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/). Check your own policy and talk to your agent, since coverage details vary by insurer and by state; this isn't a guarantee of what your specific policy pays.

That said, acting fast still matters even if flood coverage is separate. Homeowners generally have a duty to mitigate further damage once water is discovered, and documenting the loss, with photos and a written mitigation scope, helps regardless of which policy ends up paying. [The Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) has a plain-language breakdown of how flood exclusions typically work if you want to read your own declarations page with better context.

## What should you throw away after floodwater gets in?

Porous items that sat in Category 3 water for more than a few hours are usually not salvageable: carpet and carpet pad, upholstered furniture, mattresses, particleboard furniture, and drywall or insulation below the waterline. Food that touched floodwater, including sealed containers with damaged seals, should be discarded.

Hard, nonporous items like tile, metal, glass, and solid wood furniture can often be cleaned and disinfected rather than replaced. A restoration crew can tell you room by room what's worth keeping and what isn't, which saves money compared to guessing and losing something that could have been saved, or keeping something that shouldn't be.
