## Who do you call first: a plumber or a water cleanup crew?

You usually need both, and they do different jobs. A licensed plumber locates and repairs the broken pipe itself, whether that's a supply line, a slab leak, or a split fitting from a freeze event. ACS Enterprise does not perform plumbing repairs. What ACS Enterprise does is everything that happens once the water is already in your home: extraction, moisture mapping, structural drying, and getting your floors and walls back to a dry, stable condition.

If the water is still actively flowing, shutting off the main or the fixture valve comes first, by you or by a plumber, before any cleanup crew can do productive work. ## What should a water cleanup crew do in the first hour after a burst pipe?

A competent crew extracts standing water first, then maps moisture before setting a single piece of drying equipment. That order matters: you can't dry what you haven't measured, and equipment placed without a moisture map often gets left running too long or in the wrong spots.

Here's what that first hour typically looks like:

- **Extraction.** Standing water is pulled with a truck-mounted or portable extractor, not shop-vacced out over an hour.
- **Moisture mapping.** A moisture meter checks drywall, subfloor, baseboards, and cabinetry to find water that's traveled beyond what you can see.
- **Containment and material assessment.** Saturated baseboards, trim, or flooring that won't dry in place get identified for removal so drying equipment isn't wasted on materials that have to come out anyway.
- **Equipment placement.** Air movers and a dehumidifier go in based on the moisture map, sized to the square footage and the amount of water involved.
- **Documentation.** Photos and moisture readings get logged from the start, which becomes the paper trail your insurance claim needs later.

A good crew follows this order roughly in line with the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage restoration standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), the industry reference for how structural drying is supposed to be measured and verified, not just eyeballed.

## What questions should you ask before signing a work authorization?

Ask exactly what's covered, what isn't, and who's paying for equipment that stays running past the first few days. A work authorization is a contract, and a vague one is how homeowners get surprised by the final invoice.

Specific questions worth asking: Does this document list a defined scope (rooms, materials, equipment) or is it open-ended? Will you bill my insurance directly, or do I pay and submit for reimbursement? How often will moisture readings be checked and shared with me? What happens if drying takes longer than expected, who decides that and based on what measurement?

If a company can't answer these clearly before you sign, that's a signal to keep looking, regardless of how fast they can show up.

## How does homeowners insurance handle burst pipe water damage?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage from a burst pipe, since it's an unexpected internal failure rather than gradual wear. Coverage details vary by insurer and policy, so it's worth confirming the specifics with your agent, but a sudden supply line failure is generally the kind of loss standard policies are built to cover, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).

What usually is not covered: damage from a slow, long-term leak that went unaddressed, or flood water entering from outside the home, which requires separate flood coverage. The distinction matters, so document the source of the water (a visibly burst or split line, not a drip that had been going on for weeks) as early as possible.
