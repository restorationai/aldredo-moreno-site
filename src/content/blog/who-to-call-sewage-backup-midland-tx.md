## Do I call a plumber, the City of Midland, or a restoration crew first?

Do both the cleanup call and the line call the same day: call a licensed plumber (or the City of Midland, if more than one fixture is backing up) to stop the source, and call a [sewage cleanup](/services/sewage-cleanup/) crew to handle whatever has already reached your floors, carpet, or crawlspace.

These are two different jobs. A plumber or the city clears the blockage. A restoration crew removes the contaminated water, disinfects the affected materials, and dries the structure so mold and odor don't set in behind the walls. One without the other leaves you exposed, either the line backs up again or the sewage sits long enough to soak into subfloors and drywall.

If the backup is still actively rising, a single fixture overflowing (usually a toilet) points to a private line clog, which is a plumber's job. If the lowest drain in the house, a floor drain, a basement drain, or a utility sink, is pushing up sewage while multiple fixtures gurgle at once, that's often the city main, and Midland's utility department needs the call.

## How do I know if it's my line or the city's main?

In Midland, the sewer line from your house to the property line, or to the city's main in the street, is your responsibility. From that connection point outward, it's the city's. A clogged private lateral usually shows up as one slow or backed-up fixture; a main line problem usually shows up as several fixtures backing up at once, often starting with the lowest drain in the house.

Older pier-and-beam homes around Midland complicate this. If your house sits on piers with a crawlspace underneath, a lateral break under the house can push sewage into the crawlspace without ever showing up as a visible backup indoors. If you smell sewage under the house, notice damp soil through a foundation vent, or see standing water through a crawlspace access door, treat it as an active backup even if nothing inside has overflowed yet.

When you're not sure which line is at fault, call the plumber first. A plumber can usually tell within minutes whether the clog is on your side or the city's, and hand you off to the right department if it isn't.

## What do I do in the first hour?

Stop using every drain and toilet in the house until the source is identified. That single step prevents a bad backup from becoming a flooded one.

If a toilet is actively overflowing, shut off the water supply valve at its base (turn it clockwise) rather than flushing again. Keep kids and pets out of any room with standing water or wastewater odor; sewage backups carry Category 3 water, also called black water, under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), and it's not something anyone should wade through. Don't run the dishwasher, washing machine, or any other drain-connected appliance until you know whether the problem is isolated to one fixture or the whole system.

If the water has already reached carpet, baseboards, or a crawlspace, that part of the job belongs to a cleanup crew, not a mop and a bucket. Sewage that sits for more than a few hours starts soaking into porous materials in ways a wet-vac can't fully reach.

## Is a sewage backup actually a health hazard?

Yes. Raw sewage can carry bacteria, parasites, and viruses, and the [CDC's guidance on cleaning safely after a disaster](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/safety/index.html) is clear that contaminated water needs protective equipment and proper disposal, not a DIY mop job.
