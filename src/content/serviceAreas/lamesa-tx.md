## Restoration emergencies common in Lamesa

Lamesa sits on the South Plains of West Texas, where the weather swings hard between extremes. Summers bring intense thunderstorms that can dump a large volume of rain in a short window, and the area's flat terrain and clay-heavy soil mean that water doesn't soak in fast. It pools against foundations, backs up into yards, and finds its way into slabs and crawlspaces faster than homeowners expect. Winter brings the opposite problem: hard freezes that catch exposed or poorly insulated pipes, especially in older homes with galvanized supply lines, leading to burst pipes and sudden interior flooding once the thaw starts. Add in the occasional hailstorm that compromises roofing and siding, letting wind-driven rain into attics and wall cavities, and you get a town where water damage shows up from both ends: below, through soil and slab, and above, through storm-damaged building envelopes.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Lamesa

Much of Lamesa's housing dates to the mid-20th century, with slab-on-grade construction dominant in newer sections and older pier-and-beam homes scattered through the town's original core. Plumbing in that older stock often means galvanized supply lines and cast iron drains, both of which corrode internally over decades and fail without warning, especially after a freeze event stresses the system.

The soil across Dawson County runs heavy on clay and caliche, which expands and contracts with moisture swings. That movement stresses slab foundations and can open gaps around plumbing penetrations that let water travel under a home instead of draining away from it. Structural repair tied to a water loss, where framing or subflooring needs replacement, typically requires a permit through the City of Lamesa, and homeowners should expect that step if the damage goes beyond surface materials.
